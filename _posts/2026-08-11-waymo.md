Riding my bicycle through the Mission District of San Francisco, I've got the same protocol at every intersection, with every vehicle I encounter: peer through the windshield, find the face, and try to catch the eyes. Sometimes I get a courteous wave. Sometimes an expressionless stare. But more often than I'd like, the eyes are down—at a phone, presumably—and I have to figure out when and how to proceed so that I don't get hit.

42% good, 18% bad, and a big fat 39% sitting in the middle with mixed feelings. The Chronicle then breaks the results down by demographic—Progressive, Liberal, Moderate, Conservative, Male, Female, College-educated, No college degree—but I'd like to see it sliced by **Runner**, **Cyclist**, and **¬Runner/Cyclist**, because any San Franciscan who runs or rides through the city would rather share the road with a fleet of Waymos than with the average set of motorists, in a heartbeat. And I suspect very few of them would land in that 39% middle, because the middle isn't where you sit after you've had a close call.

The peer-reviewed safety record is lopsided in Waymo's favor: 82% fewer injury crashes involving cyclists, 92% fewer involving pedestrians, 82% fewer involving motorcyclists, measured against human drivers in the same places.[^1]

There are good drivers out there (mil gracias), but most of us famously overestimate our talent.[^2] And look, bumping a Model T down an empty gravel road was one thing, but we aren't built with the reaction time or the processing bandwidth for *today's* traffic complexity.

But the deeper problem isn't the average driver at all, because cycling safety isn't a function of the average driver; rather it's a function of the worst one. Ninety-nine attentive drivers don't protect you from the hundredth.

None of which means the AVs are there yet. There are real [gaps](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/10/technology/waymo-expansion-edge-cases.html?smid=tw-share) left to close, and one of the sharpest complaints about robotaxis in this city comes from cyclists, not from Luddites: Waymo has told the SF Bicycle Coalition that pulling into bike lanes for pickups and dropoffs is "normal practice," and that keeping out of them is "too high a bar." A cyclist is currently suing after she was doored by an exiting passenger in a marked lane and thrown into a second Waymo blocking the same lane. But notice that failure isn't perception or judgment or reaction time; it's a (changeable) *product decision*—which is precisely why I'm bullish: because a defect fix improves the entire Waymo fleet at once whereas there's no equivalent patch for our worst drivers; there's only a reprimand issued after somebody's already dead.

(Also, yes, we must acknowledge that drivers will lose jobs. That's a real cost and I don't mean to wave it away in a parenthetical; it's just not apropos of this post.)

You can't make eye contact with a Waymo, but of course you don't need to, because it's already more reliable than its human counterpart, which makes you realize that eye contact is just a mediocre proxy for reliability, an attempt to infer a stranger's intention through the glass at 20 mph.[^3]

Time to hand over the keys, folks. Jeeves is electric now, and isn't looking at his phone.
